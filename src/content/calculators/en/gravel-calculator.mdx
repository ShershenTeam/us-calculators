## How to use this gravel calculator

1. **Pick a shape for the first area** — rectangle for a driveway or pad, circle for a fire pit or tree ring, triangle for a corner, or enter a total area you already know.
2. **Enter the dimensions and depth.** Feet and inches are the default; type `12'6"` and the field understands it. Tap *m / cm* for metric.
3. **Add more areas** if the project is not one simple shape: an L-shaped driveway is two rectangles; a path plus a patio are two areas. Totals are added for you.
4. **Choose the gravel type.** The density it uses is shown next to the result. If your supplier gives a different figure, choose *Custom density* and type it in.
5. **Read the result:** cubic yards to order (with the extra percentage included), U.S. tons, number of bags, and cost if you entered a price. Open *How it's calculated* to see every step with your numbers.

## Gravel formula: from square feet to cubic yards and tons

The calculation has three steps:

1. **Area (sq ft)** = length × width for a rectangle, π × (diameter ÷ 2)² for a circle, ½ × base × height for a triangle.
2. **Volume (cu yd)** = area × depth (in) ÷ 12 ÷ 27. Dividing by 12 converts inches to feet; dividing by 27 converts cubic feet to cubic yards, because a cubic yard is 3 × 3 × 3 ft.
3. **Weight (tons)** = cubic yards × density in tons per cubic yard. Density in tons per cubic yard equals density in lb/ft³ × 27 ÷ 2,000.

**Worked example.** A 10 ft × 10 ft pad, 3 in deep, crushed stone at 105 lb/ft³:

- Area: 10 × 10 = 100 sq ft
- Volume: 100 × 3 ÷ 12 = 25 cu ft = 25 ÷ 27 = **0.93 cu yd**
- With 10 % extra: 0.93 × 1.10 = **1.02 cu yd** → order 1 to 1.5 yd³
- Weight: 27.5 cu ft × 105 lb/ft³ = 2,888 lb = **1.44 tons**
- Bags: 27.5 ÷ 0.5 = **55 bags** of 0.5 cu ft

The same volume works for any loose material; only the density changes. For a plain volume without weight, use the <Calc slug="cubic-yards-calculator">cubic yards calculator</Calc>.

## Gravel weight by type: tons per cubic yard

Suppliers quote weight by the ton, so the density matters. These are ordering densities for loose, delivered material; compacted material in place is denser. The #57 figure sits between the DOT laboratory values (81 lb/ft³ loose, 92 lb/ft³ rodded) and the 1.4–1.5 t/yd³ that quarries use for ordering.

| Gravel type | lb per cu ft | Tons per cu yd | Cu yd per ton |

Moisture adds weight: wet gravel can weigh 5–10 % more than the same stone dry. If a quote seems high after rain, that is usually why.

## Yards or tons? Bags or bulk?

**Yards vs. tons.** Landscape supply yards often sell by the cubic yard because they load with a bucket; quarries sell by the ton because trucks cross a scale. Both are fine — just compare quotes in the same unit. The calculator converts between them using the density shown, and the *Suggested order* figure rounds up to the next half yard, which is the smallest increment most yards deliver.

**Bags vs. bulk.** A 0.5 cu ft bag holds about 50 lb of gravel; 54 of them make one cubic yard. Bags make sense below roughly half a yard, or when a truck cannot reach the spot. Above one yard, bulk delivery is almost always cheaper even with a delivery fee, and it saves carrying 50+ bags. Typical U.S. prices in 2026 run $40–$80 per cubic yard (or $30–$60 per ton) for common crushed stone in bulk, versus $4–$8 per 0.5 cu ft bag — that is $215–$430 per yard bagged.

**Truck sizes.** A single-axle dump truck carries about 5–7 tons (4–5 yd³ of gravel); a tandem carries 10–14 tons. If your order lands just above a truck's capacity, trimming the depth slightly or adding a second small area later can save a second delivery fee.
