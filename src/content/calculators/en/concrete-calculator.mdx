## How to calculate concrete

Concrete is ordered by volume — **cubic yards** for ready-mix, **bags** for small jobs. The calculation is the same for both:

1. **Find the area** of the pour in square feet: length × width for a rectangle, π × (diameter ÷ 2)² for a round column or post hole.
2. **Convert the thickness to feet** — divide inches by 12 (4 in = 0.333 ft).
3. **Multiply** area × thickness to get cubic feet.
4. **Divide by 27** for cubic yards, or **divide by the bag yield** for bags.

**Worked example.** A patio 10 ft × 10 ft, 4 in thick:

- Volume: 10 × 10 × 4 ÷ 12 = 33.3 ft³ = 33.3 ÷ 27 = **1.23 yd³**
- With 5 % extra: 33.3 × 1.05 = 35 ft³ = **1.30 yd³**
- In 80 lb bags: 35 ÷ 0.60 = 58.3 → **59 bags**, weighing 4,720 lb

Add every part of the job as its own pour — a slab, the footings under it, the steps up to it — and the calculator totals them. For an odd-shaped slab, choose *L-shape*, *Triangle* or *Known area*; the <Calc slug="square-footage-calculator">square footage calculator</Calc> can work out the area first if it has cut-outs.

## Bags of concrete per cubic yard

These yields come from QUIKRETE's data sheet for its standard Concrete Mix (No. 1101, 4,000 psi). Other brands of standard mix are close, but check the yield printed on your bag and pick the size you are buying.

| Bag | Yield per bag | Bags per cubic yard | Covers at 4 in thick |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 lb | 0.30 ft³ | 90 | 0.9 sq ft |
| 50 lb | 0.375 ft³ | 72 | 1.1 sq ft |
| 60 lb | 0.45 ft³ | 60 | 1.35 sq ft |
| 80 lb | 0.60 ft³ | 45 | 1.8 sq ft |

Bigger bags mean fewer trips to the mixer but heavier lifting: 45 bags of 80 lb for one cubic yard is 3,600 lb. The table under the calculator shows the count and total weight for every size, so you can choose before you buy.

## Slab thickness

Concrete Network gives 4 inches as the usual thickness for residential patios, walkways and driveways that carry passenger cars, and 5–6 inches where trucks or RVs will park. Going from 4 to 5 inches adds about 20 % to the concrete but nearly 50 % to the load the slab can carry. Many contractors also thicken the edges of a driveway by 1–2 inches, where wheels load the slab hardest.

Pour the slab on a compacted gravel base — the <Calc slug="gravel-calculator">gravel calculator</Calc> works out the stone for it — and check your local building code, which may set a minimum.

## Footings, columns and post holes

Choose **Column / hole** for anything round: fence and deck post holes, sonotube piers, round footings. Enter the diameter (type `12"` for inches), set the depth unit, and put the number of identical holes under *How many*. A 12 in hole 48 in deep holds π × 0.5² × 4 = 3.14 ft³ — about 5¼ bags of 80 lb mix, so buy 6.

Continuous footings and walls are rectangles: the length of the run, the width of the footing, and its depth as the thickness. A footing 20 ft long, 12 in wide and 12 in deep is 20 ft³, or 0.74 yd³.

## Concrete steps

Choose **Stairs** and enter the number of risers, the height of each riser, the depth of each tread, the width of the flight and the depth of the landing at the top. The calculator treats the stairs as solid concrete on the ground: each step is a block reaching from the ground to its tread, and the landing runs the full height.

**volume = width × (tread × riser × N(N−1)/2 + landing × N × riser)**, where N is the number of risers.

Three risers of 7 in, 11 in treads, 4 ft wide with a 36 in landing: 4 × (0.917 × 0.583 × 3 + 3 × 3 × 0.583) = 27.4 ft³ — about 1 cubic yard, or 48 bags of 80 lb mix with 5 % extra.

## How much extra to order

Never order the exact amount. The base is never perfectly level, forms bow slightly under the weight, and some concrete stays in the mixer, the wheelbarrow or the truck chute. Running short in the middle of a pour leaves a cold joint that can crack. A margin of about **5 %** is sound on a well-prepared, compacted base; add more over uneven or soft ground. Concrete Network describes a 20-yard order that finishes with one yard to spare as a good order.
